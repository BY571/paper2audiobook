## Opening

This is RoboJEPA, Scaling Robotic Latent World Models, by Artem Zholus and colleagues at FAIR at Meta, with Mila and Polytechnique Montréal, released as a preprint in October 2026. It is a scaling study. It trains a family of action-conditioned world models for robot manipulation, from twenty-two million to eight billion parameters, on a unified mixture of twenty-three public datasets spanning twelve robot platforms, fits a scaling law to their prediction error, shows that the law predicts planning performance on both simulated and real robots, and releases all checkpoints and code.

## The problem

Scaling has been the engine of recent progress in language and vision, and what made it actionable was predictability: a scaling law tells you what another order of magnitude of compute will buy before you spend it. Robotics has no such law for world models. A world model is a learned simulator of how an environment evolves under actions, and latent world models, which predict in a learned feature space rather than in pixels, have recently shown that a robot can plan toward a goal image it has never seen, without any task-specific policy. But each such model has been trained once, at one size, on one small dataset. Nobody knows whether these models get better with scale, how fast, and whether a better prediction error means a better robot.

That matters more in robotics than elsewhere because interaction data is the scarce resource. If you know how model quality scales with compute and data, you know whether to buy more demonstrations, train a bigger model, or stop. Without that, scaling is a gamble.

There is also an engineering obstacle. Robot datasets disagree on everything: number of cameras, image resolution, frame rate, action convention, proprioceptive state. Training one model across all of them requires an architecture and a data pipeline that absorb that heterogeneity, which previous latent world models, trained on a single dataset, never had to solve.

## The idea in one breath

Keep the recipe deliberately simple so the scaling behaviour is clean. Freeze a strong pretrained video encoder, train a transformer predictor on top of it to deterministically predict the next frame's features from past features, actions and robot state, with a plain regression loss. Make the predictor consume any mix of camera views, action spaces and robot types through a single flat token stream. Train eight sizes across four orders of magnitude of compute on fifteen thousand hours of video, fit a scaling law to the held-out prediction error, and check whether that error predicts how well the same models plan when deployed on a robot.

## Where it sits

The direct ancestor is the action-conditioned predictor of V-JEPA 2, which post-trained a small predictor on a frozen video encoder using one dataset of about seventy thousand episodes and showed zero-shot planning on a Franka arm. RoboJEPA keeps that design but replaces the single dataset with a unified multi-embodiment mixture, scales the predictor to billions of parameters, and characterises the scaling, so the authors say the two are not directly comparable.

Pixel-space world models like Dreamer, Genie, Cosmos and the GAIA line reconstruct or generate video. RoboJEPA deliberately does not: predicting in a frozen encoder's feature space is what makes an eight-billion-parameter predictor affordable, because nothing is spent on rendering every pixel. A diffusion decoder is trained separately, purely to visualise what the model imagines, and is never used in planning.

Scaling laws exist for language, generative modelling and vision transformers, and a few studies report empirical scaling trends for world models in games or in driving. The authors' distinction is between a trend and a law: a trend says a metric improves with scale, a law extrapolates it and says by how much. They claim the first predictive law for action-conditioned world models on real multi-embodiment robot data, verified by holding out the two largest models.

The complementary line of work scales policies rather than world models: the large vision-language-action models trained on the same data substrate. Two of those appear in the real-robot evaluation as references, not baselines, since they are conditioned on text rather than on a goal image.

## How it works

Start with the model. A frozen encoder, V-JEPA 2.1 at the giant size, turns each camera frame into a grid of patch features. Every timestep contributes those visual features for each camera, plus one token for the action and one for the robot's proprioceptive state, and all of it is interleaved into a single flat sequence. The predictor is a vision transformer with the modern stabilisers: RMS normalisation, query-key normalisation, which the authors found essential for stability at scale, grouped-query attention, and a rotary position encoding factorised over four axes, time, height, width and camera view. A learned per-camera bias tells the model which view is which, so camera order can be shuffled in training. Each robot type gets its own small linear projection for actions and states, which is the only place embodiment identity enters; everything else is shared. Attention is causal at the level of timesteps and limited to a window of eight past steps, which keeps per-step cost constant during long rollouts and lets the model be cached like a language model.

The training objective has two halves. The first is teacher forcing: given encoded frames and actions, predict each next frame's features and penalise the absolute error against the encoder's features. The second is an autoregressive rollout: give the model only a prefix of encoded frames but the full action sequence, let it predict the rest from its own outputs, and apply the same loss. That second term is what teaches the model not to let its errors compound. During pretraining the rollout is two steps; during the final learning-rate cooldown it is extended to ten steps at mixed resolutions, which the authors show is a better use of compute at large budgets.

Now the data. Twenty-three datasets are unified into one on-disk format: DROID, RoboSet, RoboMind, LeRobot, RoboCasa, the 1X humanoid data, AgiBot World, and nine subsets of Open X-Embodiment. That is about two point nine million episodes, fifteen thousand hours of video and six thousand seven hundred hours with synchronised actions, across single arms, dual arms and humanoids. Frame rates ranging from one to sixty hertz are decimated to a common world-model rate. Actions are normalised to state deltas, end-effector pose changes plus gripper, or to chunks of raw controller commands for datasets that ship them, and the same demonstration is often presented under several action parameterisations at once, which the authors found improves the model at fixed compute. Cameras are mapped to seven semantic roles so that every left exterior camera, from any dataset, shares the same learned view bias. GPUs are partitioned into groups by number of views and resolution, with batch sizes balanced per group, so that one-view and two-view data train the same parameters in lockstep.

Eight predictor sizes are trained, from twenty-two million to eight billion parameters, differing only in depth, width and head counts, with a warmup, flat, then cooldown learning-rate schedule. The flat stage is run once per size and checkpoints along it are annealed separately, which is what makes the compute sweep affordable. Training compute is counted exactly, by measuring the floating-point operations per step for each configuration rather than using the usual approximation.

The scaling law is fit to the forward-prediction error on held-out data: nine steps of autoregressive imagination from one observation, with the error averaged over all predicted tokens against the encoder's true features. Two held-out sets are used, real DROID episodes in unseen scenes and simulated RoboCasa scenes. Four candidate curves are compared by fitting them on the six smaller models and testing how well they predict the four- and eight-billion models. The winner is a second-order power law, where the exponent itself drifts with the logarithm of compute, with extrapolation error two to three times lower than a plain power law. Both best fits put the irreducible error close to where the largest models already sit, which the authors read as the models being near saturation on this data budget.

Planning uses the cross-entropy method in feature space. Encode the current views and a single goal image once. Sample thousands of candidate action sequences, roll each through the predictor with a shared key-value cache, score each by the distance between its final imagined features and the goal features, keep the best, refit the sampling distribution, repeat, and execute the first actions of the best plan before replanning. There are no subgoals and no learned action proposal; actions are drawn from a uniform-like distribution and the world model alone ranks them. To make this fast enough for a real robot, the predictor is compiled ahead of time into a fixed set of static programs, which cuts cold start from fifteen minutes to under two seconds and removes most dispatch overhead, and planning is spread over several GPUs. A planning step for the eight-billion model takes a few seconds.

## Experiments

There are four kinds of evaluation.

First, the scaling law itself, as described: eight sizes, compute from two times ten to the nineteen to nearly ten to the twenty-three floating-point operations, prediction error on DROID and RoboCasa held-out sets, extrapolation checked on the two largest models.

Second, greedy planning in simulation. Custom RoboCasa kitchen environments, in held-out kitchens, with a blocking controller: reaching a target end-effector pose, and reaching while holding a grasped banana, can or mug. These can be solved by moving toward the goal at every step. Every checkpoint along every training run is evaluated by closed-loop planning, giving success curves against compute.

Third, non-greedy planning in simulation. Reaching around an appliance placed between start and goal, and pushing a flour bag across a counter with a closed gripper, where start and goal robot poses are identical so only the object's motion can change the scene. These require multi-step lookahead. The authors first found that models trained with the two-step rollout showed only a noisy relation between compute and success, then retrained the cooldown with the ten-step rollout and re-ran.

Fourth, the real robot. A Franka arm on the DROID platform with one external camera and one wrist camera, controlled through blocking end-effector translation plus gripper, orientation fixed. Three tasks of increasing difficulty: grasp a cup, lift it and hold it until the end of the episode, and pick and place it at a target. All eight model sizes are evaluated, thirty episodes each per task, with identical planning hyperparameters across sizes and tasks. The goal is a single image obtained by moving the robot to the goal pose and placing the cup in its gripper. Two vision-language-action models, pi zero FAST and pi zero point five, are run on the same tasks with text instructions and fifty episodes each as references. Episodes are scored by hand for task progress and binary success.

What is not tested. The encoder is frozen throughout, so the law describes a predictor scaling on a fixed representation, not a jointly trained system. Data scale is never varied independently of compute; every model sees the same mixture, so there is no law in data, only in compute. The real-robot tasks are one platform, one object, three short tabletop tasks, with thirty episodes per cell and no error bars. The planner acts in translation and gripper only, with orientation fixed, and plans toward a single goal image, so no language goals and no subgoals. The vision-language-action references are run under a different interface and the authors say they are not comparable. No comparison is made to any other world model at matched compute. And the authors note that training for multiple epochs on a fixed corpus, with fits already near saturation, means further gains will likely need more diverse data rather than parameters alone.

## Results

The scaling law holds. On both held-out sets the compute-optimal frontier follows the second-order power law, and a fit on the six smaller models predicts the four- and eight-billion models within roughly a thousandth of the error metric. The decoded imaginations show what that buys: at two billion and above, rollouts keep multiple camera views consistent and preserve gripper-object contact and folding structure that smaller models blur away. The eight-billion model, fine-tuned on DROID at seven hundred and twenty p, imagines all three camera views at once and transfers content between them.

Greedy planning in simulation improves predictably with compute, with larger models forming a clean frontier. End-effector control emerges around ten to the twenty operations and holding an object around three times that, both reachable with fifty to one hundred million parameters. The authors' recommendation follows: define the downstream capability you need, then estimate the minimal compute that reaches it.

Non-greedy planning is where training recipe matters. With short-rollout training the obstacle task is noisy and smaller models sometimes beat larger ones. With the ten-step rollout, the long-horizon loss also lowers the irreducible error and the obstacle task falls into a clear frontier, taking off at ten to the twenty-one operations and saturating at ten to the twenty-two. The push task is the striking one: no model of any size succeeds until about ten to the twenty-two operations, after which the two-, four- and eight-billion models all begin to succeed, reaching about half. So capabilities arrive in a natural order: end-effector control, then holding an object, then static scene geometry, then object dynamics, each at its own compute threshold.

On the real robot, success on all three tasks trends upward with model size. Grasp goes from about thirty percent at the smallest sizes to sixty-seven at eight billion. Lift goes from near zero to fifty. Pick and place is hardest, peaking at thirty-one percent at two billion and sitting at twenty-seven at eight billion. The vision-language-action references do best on pick and place, where pi zero point five reaches fifty-three percent, but collapse on lift, at zero, which the authors attribute to the verb being rare in DROID text instructions. Qualitatively, small models execute smoothly but cannot recover from a bad approach, while models from two billion up adapt and retry; smaller models also tend to open the gripper after grasping and drop the object.

## Conclusion and downsides

The main contribution is the method of the study rather than any single number: a clean, reproducible scaling setup for robot world models, with exact compute accounting, four candidate laws tested by extrapolation, and the link from offline prediction error to online planning closed on both a simulator and a real arm. The emergence thresholds for distinct capabilities, and the finding that spending cooldown compute on longer rollouts improves the law itself, are practically useful. The engineering appendices, on data unification and on getting a billion-parameter predictor to plan in seconds, are unusually complete, and everything is released.

The limitations are real and most are stated by the authors.

First, this is a law for a predictor on a frozen encoder, and the appendix shows how much that matters: on the earlier V-JEPA 2 encoder the same predictor collapsed or diverged beyond a certain size, and the whole study only worked once the encoder was swapped for one with better dense features. The law is conditional on that choice, and joint scaling of encoder and predictor is untested.

Second, compute and data are confounded. There is one data mixture, and the fits already sit close to their irreducible floor. The authors say the next gains will need more diverse data; the paper cannot say how much, because it never varied data.

Third, the real-robot evaluation is small and narrow. Thirty episodes per cell with no confidence intervals, one arm, one cup, translation-only control, and a planner with no learned proposal. The upward trend is visible, but the pick-and-place curve is non-monotonic and the eight-billion model is not best on it.

Fourth, the comparisons to policies are explicitly not comparisons. Different goal specification, different controller, different data regime. A reader should take the vision-language-action numbers as context and nothing more.

Fifth, the planner is expensive. Thousands of forward passes per decision, several GPUs, seconds per step even after heavy compilation work. The paper is candid that this is not real-time, and the control loop is blocking, so the robot waits for the plan.

Sixth, the irreducible floor is a property of this encoder and this evaluation, not of the world. Prediction error against a frozen encoder's features saturating does not tell you how much of what matters for manipulation that encoder captured in the first place.

The open problems the authors name are training the encoder jointly, establishing a data scaling law, adding language goals, and adding a learned action proposal to the planner. The one I would add is a controlled comparison to a pixel-space world model at matched compute, since the paper's argument for latent prediction rests on cost, and the cost side is never measured against the alternative.

## Recap

The problem was that nobody knew whether robotic world models improve predictably with scale, or whether better prediction error means a better robot. The approach is a deliberately simple action-conditioned predictor on a frozen video encoder, trained at eight sizes on a unified twenty-three-dataset mixture, with a scaling law fit to held-out prediction error and tested by extrapolation to the largest models. It works through a flat token stream that absorbs any mix of cameras, action spaces and robot types, a two-part loss of teacher forcing plus autoregressive rollout, exact compute accounting, and planning by sampling action sequences and ranking them with the world model in feature space. Prediction error follows a second-order power law in compute on both real and simulated held-out data, planning success in simulation improves predictably with distinct capabilities emerging at distinct compute thresholds, and on a real Franka arm success rises with model size from about thirty to sixty-seven percent on grasping. The main downsides are that the law is conditional on a frozen encoder whose choice made or broke the study, compute and data are never separated, the real-robot evaluation is thirty episodes per cell on one arm and one object, and the planner needs several GPUs and seconds per step. Read the full paper if you train or deploy world models; the scaling-law methodology, the data unification appendix and the inference engine appendix are the parts worth your time.
