## Opening

This is Recova, Agent-Guided Failure Recovery for Autonomous Robotic Manipulation, by Isabella Liu, An-Chieh Cheng and colleagues at NVIDIA, UC San Diego and UT Austin, released as a preprint in October 2026. It is a systems paper. It proposes a framework in which a coding agent, a language model that writes and runs code, builds a simulated copy of a robot's workstation, discovers how the robot's task policy fails there, develops recovery behaviours for those failures, and then runs a deployment loop on real robots that detects failures, invokes recoveries, and asks a human only when nothing else works.

## The problem

Vision-language-action models, the large policies that map camera images and a text instruction to robot motions, can now fold laundry, pack boxes and tidy rooms. But a small mistake can stop an otherwise capable policy dead. A cup tips over and has to be set upright before pouring can continue. A pencil lands across the rim of a box and has to be moved before the lid will close. In both cases the task is still doable, but the policy cannot continue until the scene is fixed.

The reason is in the training data. Almost all of it consists of successful trajectories that start from a tidy initial state. There is little or nothing showing how to restore a scene that has gone wrong. Failures create a long tail of odd configurations, and fixing them may mean undoing a previous action, moving an object out of the way, or temporarily moving away from the goal. Simply retrying the task policy usually leaves the obstruction in place.

Learning recovery behaviours directly on hardware is expensive. You have to provoke failures on purpose, reset the scene by hand each time, and risk breaking things. A reconstructed simulation of the workstation, a digital twin, is a safer place to explore, but recovery behaviours still have to be checked against real contacts and real sensing. So what is needed is a loop: discover behaviours in simulation, verify them on the robot, and feed the physical experience back into learning.

The authors argue that language-model agents are the natural coordinators of such a loop, since recent models combine image understanding with the ability to run code and revise it from feedback. The hard part is joining these abilities into one complete process: diagnose a failure, pick or develop a correction, verify it worked, keep the solution, and route the resulting data to whichever policy should learn from it.

## The idea in one breath

Treat scene recovery as its own capability, separate from task execution, with its own policy and its own data. Let a coding agent build a digital twin of the workstation, try the task there, and for each failure write a small recovery program and collect corrective rollouts. Use those to seed a task policy, a recovery policy, and a library of reusable recovery programs. Then deploy on the real robot with a vision-language monitor that watches for failures, triggers a recovery, verifies the scene is workable again, and calls a human only when no known recovery applies. Every real success, verified recovery and human demonstration goes back into training the policy it belongs to, so human intervention drops round by round.

## Where it sits

Recova draws on three strands of prior work and combines them. Code-as-policy agents, like CaP-Agent, RATs and ASPIRE, use a language model to write executable robot programs and accumulate skills through self-directed exploration. Recova uses the same idea for the recovery library, but pairs it with a learned task policy rather than programming everything.

Failure detection and recovery is a crowded area. Prior work detects failures with fine-tuned vision-language models, monitors execution against written constraints, or predicts failures from policy uncertainty. Separate recovery controllers also have precedent: Recovery RL, RecoveryChaining, FARL and RACER all learn recovery behaviour alongside a task controller in some form, and FailSafe generates corrective data for a vision-language model. What Recova adds is the connection of recovery development in a reconstructed scene to a deployment loop with learned and programmed executors, visual restoration checks, and human demonstrations routed to separate policies.

The third strand is learning from deployment. DAgger aggregates expert labels on states the learner visits, and human-gated or robot-gated variants let an operator take over or ask for help. ENPIRE lets an agent manage resets and rollouts on real robots. Recova's version requests human input only at failures, not at every visited state, and sends task successes, task demonstrations and recovery demonstrations to the policy each one teaches.

## How it works

Start with the two capabilities. A task policy takes camera images, robot state and a language instruction such as "stack the rings in size order", and acts until a completion condition holds. A recovery skill is named by a short recovery instruction such as "push the stuck ring down", and is carried out either by a learned recovery policy conditioned on that instruction, or by a program from a skill library that composes scene queries with motion primitives. Conditioning on the recovery instruction rather than the task instruction is what makes a skill reusable: wherever the same failure recurs, the same skill applies. Recovery skills play three roles. Restorative skills return the scene to a workable state, such as righting a fallen object. Enabling skills prepare the next step, such as moving an object within reach. Adaptive skills change the approach rather than repeat it, such as re-grasping with a different pose.

Next, the digital twin. A coding agent rebuilds the workstation as a MuJoCo scene from real robot recordings: camera images, robot trajectories, and camera calibration when available. Starting from models of the robot and the task objects, the agent fits object poses and contact parameters so that replaying a recorded real trajectory in the twin lines up with the real camera views. That grounds the twin in the real workstation's scale and layout.

In the twin, the agent attempts the task. Successful attempts become the task dataset. When an attempt fails, the agent diagnoses the failure, names a recovery instruction, and develops the skill in two forms at once: a program for the skill library, and recovery rollouts pairing the disturbed scene and the instruction with corrective actions. Those rollouts form the recovery dataset. So before the robot is ever deployed, both policies have initial training data and the skill library has entries.

Then the real-robot loop, which is run by a vision-language monitor in four steps. Monitor: before the first attempt, the monitor turns the task instruction and the initial images into an explicit, checkable completion condition, and then periodically checks whether it holds. Detect: at a finer interval, an intervention query compares recent frames with the initial scene and decides whether to step in. It also reports whether the scene is still intact and names a recovery instruction, reusing an existing one from the library whenever it fits. Decide: if the scene is intact but the policy has stalled, the human gives a task demonstration. Otherwise the recovery policy runs the named instruction within a time budget, provided that instruction is in its registry of trained skills. If no learned skill applies or the attempt fails, the human demonstrates the recovery, and that segment is labelled with the instruction. After two consecutive interventions on one task, the next attempt is a full human demonstration, to break cycles of repeated failure. Verify: after an autonomous recovery, a restoration query checks the current scene against the initial images. Objects do not need to return to their exact positions, only to an equivalently workable arrangement. Once verified, the robot returns home and the task policy starts a fresh attempt.

Because humans are only needed at these handoffs, one operator can supervise several stations with different scene setups at once. In the paper, four stations run in parallel. Meanwhile the agent keeps developing and testing new recovery programs in the twin as new failures turn up, so the skill library keeps growing.

Finally, learning across rounds. During a round both policies stay fixed while data accumulates in four groups: task rollouts completed without takeover, human task demonstrations, human recovery demonstrations labelled with their instruction, and autonomous recoveries that passed the restoration check. Failed policy segments are kept for analysis but excluded from training. At the end of the round, successes and task demonstrations go into the task dataset, and recovery demonstrations plus verified recoveries go into the recovery dataset. Each policy is fine-tuned on its own data with its ordinary supervised objective. When a human demonstrates a recovery for an instruction that had no learned skill, that demonstration seeds the skill, the instruction is added to the library, and once the recovery policy has trained on it, the instruction enters the registry so the robot can do it alone next time.

The monitor in the real experiments is Gemini 3.8 Flash, viewing a composite of four cameras and answering four prompt templates in JSON. Completion checks run every twenty seconds, intervention checks every ten, and each autonomous recovery attempt is capped at fifty seconds. All task and recovery policies are fine-tuned from the pi zero point five base model with the same recipe, predicting fifty-step chunks of joint-position targets at thirty hertz, in stages of a few thousand steps on eight GPUs as data arrives.

## Experiments

Two simulation benchmarks and four real-robot tasks.

In simulation, LIBERO-Pro is a stress-tested version of a standard manipulation benchmark with object, goal and spatial suites, each run with initial-position swaps and with task perturbations that change the target object, giving six settings, each evaluated on fifty initial states. MolmoSpaces spans four categories across diverse scenes: pick, pick-and-place, open, and close, the latter two involving cabinets and drawers. Here Recova pairs a MolmoAct2 task policy with programmatic recovery skills only; a recovery program takes over when the policy's budget runs out or progress stalls. Baselines are code-as-policy agents, CaP-Agent, RATs and on LIBERO-Pro ASPIRE, and vision-language-action policies including pi zero, pi zero point five, MolmoAct2 and pi zero FAST. Baseline numbers are taken from their publications; Recova's are the authors' own runs.

On real hardware, four tasks on bimanual workstations, each with two six-joint arms with parallel grippers and four cameras: packing a pencil into a box, stacking four rings on a peg in size order, drawing a mahjong tile from a wall into a hand row, and discarding a tile from the hand. In each task's twin the agent developed six or seven recovery skills, twenty-five in total. Each task is evaluated over twenty trials in three configurations: the base task policy, after DAgger fine-tuning, and with recovery skills enabled. Separately, four collection rounds on the tile-drawing task track how often a human had to take over, and a fourteen-minute session on four stations at once shows how station time was divided between task policy, recovery policy, human control and resets.

What is not tested. The simulation results use programmatic recovery only, so the learned recovery policy is only evaluated on the real tasks. The digital twin is built by the agent, but the paper does not measure how faithful the twins are or how often twin-developed skills fail to transfer. There are no ablations of the components: no run without the twin, without the monitor's verification step, or without separating the two policies. The intervention-rate study is on a single task with seven or eight episodes per round. Every real task is tabletop, bimanual, with rigid objects, on one robot platform with one base model and one monitor model. No deformable objects, no mobile manipulation, and no comparison of the vision-language monitor's accuracy against ground truth. Trials per real configuration are twenty, judged by an operator.

## Results

On LIBERO-Pro, Recova reaches a mean success of about seventy-nine percent across the six settings, about seven points above ASPIRE, the strongest baseline. The gains concentrate in the goal and spatial suites under task perturbation, where Recova leads ASPIRE by over twenty points; ASPIRE stays ahead on the object suite. The vision-language-action baselines collapse in this benchmark, with pi zero at zero across the board and MolmoAct2 around a third.

On MolmoSpaces, Recova leads in all four categories with a mean near sixty-five percent, about twenty-seven points above RATs. Pick-and-place remains the hardest category, at roughly forty percent against thirty-two for the best baseline.

On the real robots, the base task policy averages about twenty-four percent success across the four tasks. DAgger fine-tuning more than triples that to about seventy-eight. Enabling recovery skills adds another ten points to about eighty-eight, the best result on every task, with the largest recovery gains, fifteen points each, on ring stacking and pencil packing, where a single misplaced object blocks the rest of the task. On the tile-drawing task, over four collection rounds, episodes completed by the task policy rose from about twelve percent to about eighty-six, while human takeovers fell from seven of eight episodes in the first round to none of seven in the fourth. In the four-station session, the task policy completed twenty-two of thirty-one episodes and human control took about eleven percent of total station time.

## Conclusion and downsides

The design is coherent and the parts fit together well. Separating recovery from task execution, naming recoveries by instruction so they transfer between tasks, discovering them in a twin before touching hardware, and routing each kind of experience to the policy it should teach are all sensible choices, and the appendix gives the full orchestration algorithm and the monitor prompts, which is unusually complete. The intervention curve falling to zero on one task is the result the paper is really about.

Now the caveats.

First, the headline comparisons are not quite like for like. Baseline numbers in simulation come from prior publications rather than re-runs, and in the simulation benchmarks Recova is a task policy plus a recovery layer, while most baselines are a policy alone. The seven-point lead over ASPIRE on LIBERO-Pro is the one apples-to-apples comparison against another agentic skill-library system, and there ASPIRE still wins a third of the settings.

Second, there are no ablations. The paper cannot say how much comes from the twin, how much from the monitor's verification, how much from separate policies, and how much from simply doing DAgger with a human in the loop. The real-robot table suggests DAgger alone accounts for most of the gain, fifty-four points, and recovery skills for ten. That is still a real gain, but the framing puts the emphasis on the smaller part.

Third, the agent-built twin is asserted rather than measured. How well the fitted twin matches reality, and how often a twin-developed skill fails on the real robot, is exactly what decides whether this scales, and the paper does not report it.

Fourth, the intervention study is thin: one task, four rounds, seven or eight episodes each, no error bars, and no second task to show the trend is general. Twenty trials per real configuration without confidence intervals makes ten-point differences suggestive rather than firm.

Fifth, the system leans on a commercial vision-language model as monitor. Its false-positive and false-negative rates are not reported, every check costs a few seconds of latency, and the robot holds still during completion and restoration checks, which the throughput numbers absorb silently.

Sixth, the setting is narrow: rigid tabletop objects, one platform, one base model. Whether recovery instructions discovered for a pencil tray transfer to anything deformable, or to a different gripper, is untested.

The open problems the authors name are automating the registration of newly learned skills and sharing them across tasks. The one I would add is measuring twin fidelity and skill transfer, since the whole pipeline rests on it.

## Recap

The problem was that manipulation policies trained on tidy successful demonstrations cannot restore a scene after a small failure, and learning recovery on hardware is slow and risky. The approach is to make recovery its own capability with its own policy, data and skill library, developed by a coding agent in a digital twin and then verified and refined on real robots. It works through a vision-language monitor that checks completion, detects failures, triggers a learned or programmed recovery under a named instruction, verifies the scene is workable, and calls a human only when no known skill applies, with every success, verified recovery and demonstration routed back to the policy it should train. In simulation it beats the strongest baselines by seven points on one benchmark and twenty-seven on another, and on four real bimanual tasks recovery skills add ten points on top of DAgger fine-tuning to reach about eighty-eight percent, while human intervention on one task fell to zero over four rounds. The main downsides are the absence of ablations, baseline numbers taken from other papers, an unmeasured digital twin, and a single-task intervention study without error bars. Read the full paper if you run robots with a human in the loop; the orchestration algorithm and the four monitor prompts in the appendix are the parts you can use directly.
