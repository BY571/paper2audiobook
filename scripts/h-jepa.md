## Opening

This is H-JEPA, End-to-End Learning of Hierarchical World Models for Visual Planning, by Wancong Zhang and Basile Terver as equal first authors, with Michael Rabbat, Yann LeCun and Randall Balestriero, from NYU, Meta's Advanced Machine Intelligence lab, INRIA Paris and Brown, released as a preprint in October 2026. It is a methods paper with a strong analysis component. It proposes a way to train a stack of world models, each predicting further into the future in its own more abstract latent space, and to plan with them from the top down, so that a long visual task is broken into short subproblems.

## The problem

A world model learns how an environment evolves so that an agent can predict and plan. Joint-embedding predictive architectures, or JEPAs, are a family of world models that predict future states in a learned latent space rather than in pixels, which avoids wasting capacity on reconstructing detail nobody needs. Recent action-conditioned JEPAs can plan a task they were never trained on, simply by searching for actions whose predicted latent ends up close to the latent of a goal image.

But these models predict in one latent space at one timescale, and that causes two problems for long-horizon tasks. The first is compounding. A long plan means rolling the predictor forward many small steps, each adding error, and the space of action sequences to search grows with every step. The second is subtler. One shared latent has to do two jobs: carry enough fast detail to predict the next step accurately, and give a useful measure of distance to the goal. Those pull in opposite directions. A latent that encodes every joint angle of a walking robot is good for predicting the next frame, but when the goal is simply to reach a location in a maze, the distance between such latents is dominated by leg pose and says almost nothing about progress. The authors show this directly: in a maze, the distance in the lowest latent space is nearly flat everywhere except right next to the goal, so a gradient-based planner gets no signal until it is almost there.

The brain's answer, and the one hierarchical reinforcement learning and classical hierarchical control have long used, is to operate at several timescales: slow features over long strides for high-level decisions, fast features over short strides for low-level control. Hierarchical world models already exist in video prediction and in reward-driven control, but the video models reconstruct pixels and are not used for planning, the control models are tied to a task reward, and the closest task-agnostic hierarchical planner keeps all its predictors in one shared latent space, so it can split the horizon but cannot match goals at a more abstract level.

## The idea in one breath

Build a hierarchy of action-conditioned JEPAs in which each level predicts further ahead than the one below, at a coarser stride, in its own learned latent space, and train the whole stack end to end with no reward and no reconstruction. Then plan from the top down: the top level searches for a coarse plan toward the goal, its predicted states become subgoals for the level below, and so on until the bottom level produces primitive actions. When the data contains factors that change at well-separated timescales, higher levels learn to throw away the fast, unpredictable detail and keep the slow, task-relevant state, which gives them a goal cost that actually measures progress.

## Where it sits

The bottom level is LeWM, a recent single-level JEPA world model trained end to end from pixels with a regulariser called SIGReg that prevents the embeddings from collapsing to a constant. H-JEPA stacks levels on top of exactly that model, so flat LeWM is the baseline throughout.

The most important comparison is to HWM, a hierarchical planner from some of the same authors that trains predictors over several horizons but keeps them all in one shared latent space. HWM has the temporal decomposition but not the hierarchy of representations. Comparing the two isolates what distinct latent spaces add.

Other action-conditioned JEPA world models, such as DINO-WM and V-JEPA 2 with an action-conditioned head, use frozen pretrained encoders; they appear only in the real-robot evaluation as reference points. Hierarchical latent video models like Clockwork VAE learn representations at several timescales but reconstruct frames and do not plan. Model-based hierarchical control methods like Director learn hierarchies from reward. In the authors' taxonomy, H-JEPA is the only method that works from pixels, is task-agnostic, is reconstruction-free, and has a hierarchy of distinct learned latent spaces.

## How it works

Start with the single-level model. An encoder, a small vision transformer, turns each image into a latent vector, optionally concatenated with an embedding of the robot's proprioceptive state. An action encoder turns each action into an embedding. A causal transformer predictor, given a few past latents and the actions, predicts the next latent. Training uses a teacher-forced one-step prediction loss in latent space, plus SIGReg, which pushes the distribution of embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian by comparing many random one-dimensional projections against a normal distribution. Without such a term the trivial solution, where everything maps to the same point and prediction is perfect, would win.

Now stack levels on top. Each higher level has two temporal hyperparameters: the stride, which is how many lower-level steps one upper-level step advances, and the window, which is how many lower-level states one upper-level state summarises. In every experiment the stride is two and the window is one. So a level-two state is a two-layer perceptron applied to every other level-one latent, and a level-two action embedding pools the two level-one action embeddings in between, through a small transformer, into a four-dimensional macro-action. Level three does the same thing to level two. Since a level-one step spans five environment steps, level two spans ten and level three spans twenty. Each level has its own predictor, its own prediction loss and its own SIGReg term, and all levels are trained jointly from scratch, so the prediction loss of an upper level sends gradients down through the encoders below it. That last point matters for what the hierarchy learns: a lower encoder is pushed to keep whatever the upper level can predict at its own timescale.

Planning runs top down. The current observation and the goal observation are encoded at every level. The top level optimises a sequence of macro-actions, by gradient descent through its own predictor rollout, to minimise the squared distance between its final predicted latent and the goal latent at that level. Its predicted trajectory then becomes a set of subgoals for the level below. The lower level optimises its own actions so that its own predicted latents, lifted into the upper level's space through the upper encoder, match those subgoals, with the last subgoal being the goal itself. This repeats down to level one, which outputs primitive actions. In closed-loop control, only the first predicted subgoal of each upper level is matched, two blocks of level-one actions are executed, which is ten environment steps, and then everything is re-planned from the new observation. Every level uses the same gradient solver with up to ninety iterations and early stopping, and candidate action sequences at upper levels are drawn from a distribution fitted to macro-actions seen in training, since upper levels have no native action bounds.

One more piece is needed for real-world video, where the scene changes between episodes. There, a purely predictive objective has a cheap solution: encode which episode you are in, which is constant within an episode and therefore perfectly predictable, and ignore the robot, which is only predictable given actions. The authors call this slow-feature collapse and prove in the appendix that no regulariser acting on the marginal distribution of embeddings, SIGReg included, can rule it out, because a background-only encoder can satisfy any such constraint exactly. The fix is an inverse-dynamics term: a small head must regress the action from a pair of consecutive latents, which forces the latents to carry what the action changed. The term is added at every level, and the two coefficients, for SIGReg and inverse dynamics, are tuned together on a grid.

## Experiments

Four simulated environments, chosen to vary in how separated their timescales are. FourRoomDistractors is a two-dimensional navigation task in which a controlled dot moves between four rooms while a second, uncontrolled dot drifts smoothly and occasionally teleports. Visual AntMaze is a quadruped navigating a maze from a third-person camera at sixty-four by sixty-four pixels plus its joint state. Push-T is pushing a T-shaped block to a target pose. OGBench Cube is a robot arm picking and placing a cube. All use fixed cameras and backgrounds. Training data per environment is one to ten million transitions from scripted or reinforcement-learning experts, the same data for every model depth. Models with two, three and four levels are trained end to end with three seeds, against flat LeWM and against HWM built on identical code and data. Evaluation is fifty held-out start-goal tasks per environment, with success judged by the environment's own goal test, and separate task sets are used to sweep the planner's compute budget.

A fifth setting is DROID, a corpus of real teleoperated robot manipulation video in which scene, lighting, camera pose and objects change every episode. There is no simulator, so plans are scored offline by Fréchet fidelity: how closely the planned three-dimensional end-effector path follows the logged expert path, normalised so that zero is doing nothing and one hundred is the expert. The authors calibrate this metric on two simulated tasks and conclude it is a necessary condition for success and ranks progress, but does not measure how much better one planner is. Sixteen pick-and-place clips centred on the grasp are held out; the rest, about seventy-five thousand episodes, are used for training at five frames per second.

What is not tested. There is no closed-loop control on a real robot; DROID is evaluated open-loop and offline only, and the authors name this as the next step. The simulated environments are single-scene with fixed cameras, so the inverse-dynamics term is only exercised on DROID. The wrist camera in DROID is never used, only the exterior views. The depth comparison is against LeWM and HWM only, both from the authors' own code base; no other hierarchical planner is run. Strides are fixed at two and windows at one, so the abstraction schedule is never varied. Goals are always images, not language. Training compute is not matched across depths, since deeper models see fewer valid clips per epoch. Planner hyperparameters, horizons and candidate counts are tuned per environment and per depth. And the headline numbers rest on three seeds with standard errors.

## Results

In FourRoom, AntMaze and Cube, each additional level up to three shifts the success-versus-compute frontier up and to the left: higher success at lower planner cost. On Visual AntMaze, the headline, three-level H-JEPA reaches about seventy-three percent success against eighteen for flat LeWM, and about forty for HWM, with less planner compute. On FourRoom both hierarchical methods reach near one hundred percent at three levels. On Cube, H-JEPA climbs from roughly thirty to sixty at three levels and HWM to about fifty. Push-T is the exception: two levels match or beat the flat model, but three levels perform poorly even with maximum compute, which the authors trace to data. Training clips must fit within one episode, Push-T episodes are short, and a three-level model sees only fifty-eight percent of the transitions the flat model sees per epoch. Four levels degrade on Push-T and Cube for the same reason.

HWM beats flat LeWM in every environment, so temporal decomposition alone helps. H-JEPA beats HWM substantially on AntMaze and moderately on Cube, so distinct latent spaces add something on top, where the data has timescale structure.

Probes confirm the selective abstraction. On AntMaze, the ant's body state becomes steadily less recoverable from level one to level four while its global position stays fully recoverable; on FourRoom the distractor fades while the agent remains. Across environments, the amount of fast detail discarded at level two tracks the ratio between the fastest and slowest timescales in the data: large for AntMaze, FourRoom and a humanoid dataset, near zero for Push-T, Cube and DROID, which have no such gap.

The cost ablation isolates the representational effect. Keeping level-one dynamics and the level-one planner fixed, but measuring the goal cost after projecting both prediction and goal through the upper encoders, raises AntMaze success for every model, from about twenty-three to thirty-one in the two-level model, and beats the separately trained flat baseline at every depth, without any upper-level planner generating subgoals. On FourRoom the same holds; on Cube and Push-T it does not. The decomposition effect is shown separately: along expert trajectories, a level-one planner's cost to the next level-two subgoal decreases monotonically with rank correlation near one, while its cost to the final goal correlates only about half to three quarters.

On DROID, without the inverse-dynamics term every model collapses and scores zero. With it, flat LeWM reaches about thirty-four percent fidelity, HWM thirty-five, and two-level H-JEPA forty, at a lower planner budget than the flat model. Frozen-encoder reference models need orders of magnitude more compute for lower fidelity.

## Conclusion and downsides

The paper's strongest contribution is the pair of mechanisms it isolates, not the headline number. Hierarchy helps planning for two separable reasons, a better goal cost in a more abstract space and a decomposition into monotone subproblems, and the paper shows each on its own. The negative results are reported as plainly as the positive ones: no selective abstraction without a timescale gap, no benefit from projected costs on the manipulation tasks, and failure at depth on short-episode data. The DROID section is careful about its proxy metric. The appendix on slow-feature collapse is a genuine theoretical point that will outlive this particular architecture.

Now the caveats.

First, the gains are conditional on the data. The selective abstraction, and the large wins, appear where fast and slow factors are separated by a factor of three or more, which navigation tasks have and the manipulation tasks here do not. On Cube the gain is moderate, on Push-T a deeper hierarchy actively hurts, and on DROID the improvement is five points on a proxy metric. A reader interested in manipulation should weight those results, not the AntMaze headline.

Second, the hierarchy is built with a fixed stride of two and window of one at every level. That makes the abstraction schedule a doubling clock rather than something learned from the data, and the authors list variable-duration segments as future work. It also means the depth of the hierarchy and the length of training clips are coupled, which is exactly what breaks Push-T.

Third, every result is in simulation or offline. Nothing is executed on a physical robot in closed loop, and the DROID planner uses only exterior cameras, which the authors admit is the wrong choice for precise grasping.

Fourth, the comparisons are within one code base. HWM and LeWM are the authors' own, and the frozen-encoder models appear only on DROID. Whether H-JEPA beats a hierarchical model-based controller trained with reward, or a modern diffusion planner, is not asked.

Fifth, there are many tuned quantities: SIGReg and inverse-dynamics weights per level, planner horizons, candidate counts and learning rates per environment and per depth, and a compute operating point chosen on a separate task set. The paper is transparent about all of them, but transferring the method to a new environment is not a matter of flipping a switch.

Finally, the headline comparison is at matched planner compute but not matched training compute, and three seeds is few for frontier curves this close together in some environments.

The open problems the authors name are closed-loop real-robot control, learning the hierarchy from action-free video, aligning upper latents with language so goals can be words rather than images, and variable-duration upper levels. The one I would add is a principled way to choose depth from the data's timescale structure, since that structure is what the whole method depends on.

## Recap

The problem was that latent world models predict and plan in one space at one timescale, so long plans compound error and the goal cost in a detail-heavy latent gives the planner almost no signal. The approach is a stack of JEPA world models, each predicting at twice the stride of the one below in its own learned latent space, trained end to end without reward or reconstruction, and used by planning top down from coarse subgoals to primitive actions. It works because upper levels, pushed to predict over long horizons, discard fast unpredictable detail and keep slow task-relevant state, which gives a graded goal cost, while the subgoals turn a long search into short monotone ones. On Visual AntMaze a three-level model lifts success from eighteen to seventy-three percent at lower planner compute, and on real robot video it improves an offline plan-fidelity score by five points over the flat model once an inverse-dynamics term is added to prevent the encoder from collapsing onto scene identity. The main downsides are that the gains depend on the data having separated timescales, deeper hierarchies fail on short episodes, the stride schedule is fixed, nothing is tested on a real robot in closed loop, and the baselines are all from the authors' own family. Read the full paper if you work on latent world models or hierarchical planning; the cost ablation, the frequency-gap analysis and the collapse appendix are the parts worth your time.
